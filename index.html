01 / THE MISMATCH

The conversation might already be over.

To another person, “thank you” often means the work is done. It's an acknowledgment, not a request to keep going.

But a conversational interface can treat that same message as another turn. It may read the earlier exchange again and generate another reply, even if no new answer was requested.

For us, a closing gesture. For the system, perhaps a fresh task.

That small difference is what sent me looking at the research. The interesting question isn't whether being courteous is wrong. It's whether the system has learned to distinguish a request from a goodbye.

iHow do we know?⌄

What researchers measured — Delavande, Pierrard and Luccioni reported a mean of 0.245 Wh across GPU, CPU and estimated RAM energy for generated thank-you replies in a specified Llama 3.1–8B/H100 experiment.

What it does not prove — It is not a universal ChatGPT cost, a water measurement or evidence that every reply is unnecessary.

ACT 02 / THE COURTESY PARADOXTHE COURTESY PARADOX

THE SAME WORDS DON'T WORK THE SAME WAY EVERYWHERE

Does being polite
change the answer?

A thank-you after we're finished can invite another response. A polite request made during the work changes the task's wording. What does that change actually do?

We tend to imagine that courtesy is a simple setting: turn it up and people—and perhaps machines—will respond better. But language models don't necessarily follow that rule.

In a 2026 research preprint, five models were evaluated across English, Hindi and Spanish using different ways of phrasing requests. The results depended on which model, language and form of politeness the researchers examined.

Consider one English-language comparison for GPT-4o Mini. Under the study's scoring method, a direct request received a score of 0.636. A positively polite request received 0.567.

Those numbers aren't accuracy percentages. They're scores produced by the researchers' combined evaluation method. Still, the contrast complicates a familiar assumption: more polite wording does not always produce a higher evaluated score.

And this is only one pair of results. The fuller picture is more uneven.

ACT 03 / THE VALUE QUESTIONWHAT COUNTS AS EFFICIENT?

A BETTER QUESTION THAN WHO WROTE LESS

The cheapest answer
isn't always the best one.

What if the first reply is shorter—but doesn't actually finish the job?

Imagine asking two assistants to help you write an important message. The first responds immediately with something brief. It looks efficient until you notice that it misses a detail. You ask again. Then you ask for one more correction.

The second assistant gives a more complete answer. It takes longer to read, but it may be ready to use. Which approach consumed fewer resources? And which one helped you more?

We can't answer either question by looking at the first response alone. We'd need to follow the entire interaction, check whether the work was actually completed, and measure what the retries cost. The research in this experience does not contain that joint measurement.

This changes the measure I would want a Data & AI leader to use. Not simply energy per response, but resources used to reach a verified useful outcome. We would also need to account for accuracy, accessibility and the cost of review.

A long answer is not automatically better; nor is a short one automatically efficient. The real unit of progress may be the task, not the message.

iBehind this idea⌄

Evidence status — These conversation pathways are explicitly hypothetical. The proposed measure, resources per verified useful resolution, is a research and product-design question, not a value established by the politeness experiments.

How to test it — Measure the whole task across turns, record retries and actual resource use, verify completion independently, and compare quality across languages and user needs.

ACT 04 / THE LANGUAGE DIVIDETHE LANGUAGE DIVIDE

WHAT THE AVERAGE LEAVES OUT

Not every language
gets the same deal.

If an AI system appears efficient on average, does that tell us what happens when someone uses it in a different language?

In one published benchmark, researchers measured the computation associated with outputs in 122 languages. Each language had its own result. The differences weren't random decoration around a single typical cost: some conditions required substantially more energy per generated token than others.

We can learn more by starting with the whole population, then changing how we look at it. The median tells us where the middle of the distribution lies. The mean is pulled toward unusually expensive observations. Both are useful; neither is everyone's experience.

For this Qwen3–8B benchmark, the energy median is about 0.132 J per output token, while the mean is about 0.173 J. The mean is roughly 31% higher, suggesting that the “average” hides an expensive tail.

And computation isn't the only thing to examine. A scoring rule can change how capable a system appears. That's why a language-accuracy comparison should be read alongside the evaluation method, not mistaken for a judgment about a community.

Change the model, and the energy profile can change again. The complete research experience includes a separate twelve-language, three-model comparison. It is another study view—not evidence of the energy cost of politeness.

ACT 05 / THE PHYSICAL WORLDWHERE DOES THE COMPUTATION HAPPEN?

THE CLOUD ISN'T PLACELESS

Where does
the work go?

Every response depends on physical systems. But the location of a person asking a question isn't necessarily the location of the machines doing the work.

We talk about AI as though an answer comes from nowhere. In reality, a generated reply requires equipment and electricity. Depending on the system and its surroundings, there may also be water-related consequences from cooling and power generation.

There are at least two places in this story: where someone types a request, and where the computing infrastructure processes it. Those places may be far apart. For an everyday conversation, we generally cannot determine the exact facility from the prompt alone.

That missing information matters. Without it, a precise-looking map from a person's keyboard to a particular data centre would be an attractive invention—not evidence.

Two published energy numbers illustrate why we need to be careful. One Llama/H100 experiment reported a mean of about 0.245 Wh for generated thank-you replies under its own setup. A separate Google-scale production study reported 0.24 Wh for a median Gemini Apps text prompt, alongside a water estimate using its own accounting method.

The figures look almost identical. Yet they're about different work, different systems and different measurement boundaries. It would be misleading to attach the Google study's water estimate to the thank-you experiment simply because the energy figures appear close.

The lesson is not that environmental effects are unknowable. It's that credible estimates require a declared boundary: which model, which workload, which infrastructure, which measurement method and, for water, which local context.

iHow do we know?⌄

Study A — Delavande, Pierrard and Luccioni report 0.245 Wh for the specific Llama/H100 thank-you experiment.

Study B — Elsworth et al. report 0.24 Wh for a median Gemini Apps text prompt and 0.26 mL of associated water under their full-stack accounting. Neither value establishes the other study's footprint.

Unknown — This project has no facility-level routing observation for an individual chat. Water consumption, withdrawal and local scarcity should not be treated as interchangeable.

ACT 06 / THE DESIGN CHOICERETURN TO THE BEGINNING

SHOULD WE CHANGE OUR MANNERS—OR OUR MACHINES?

The words stayed
the same.

A human types “Thank you.” What happens next is also a design decision.

After all these comparisons, it's tempting to invent a new rule of etiquette. Use fewer words. Skip the please. Don't thank the machine. But the research does not establish a universal rule like that—and it would be a strange conclusion to make people less human in order to accommodate a system.

Instead, imagine three ways an AI product could handle the same message. A generated reply might feel warm or helpful. A lightweight acknowledgment might provide closure without another full message. Quiet closure might respect a user's intent to finish—or leave someone wondering whether they were heard.

These are design possibilities, not measured energy-saving strategies. A good comparison would test whether people understand the behaviour, whether assistance is still available when needed, and what the system actually consumes.

ONE HUMAN MESSAGE / THREE POSSIBLE DESIGNSHYPOTHESES, NOT A RANKING
THE MESSAGE DOES NOT CHANGE
Thank you.
You're welcome.

A new generated message appears.

Possible benefit: A friendly reply may reassure the reader or sustain the conversation.

Possible risk: It can generate another turn when none was wanted.

What to test: Comprehension, task closure, accessibility and actual resource consumption.

All three options are conceptual designs. Their relative energy, user satisfaction and task outcomes have not been measured here. Quiet closure does not necessarily mean zero computation.

Notice what didn't change: the person's words. What changed was the interface's interpretation of them. The more meaningful research question is whether the next response adds something useful—and how we would know.

Perhaps the real cost of courtesy is not in the courtesy itself, but in the gap between what people mean and what the systems we build decide to do.

iWhat evidence would decide this?⌄

What is established — Published experiments report specific response-energy and language/model differences under defined conditions. They do not compare these three interface endings.

What to test — Randomized, multilingual usability and resource measurements comparing genuine task closure, comprehension, accessibility, rework, user preference and electricity use. Preserve help-seeking and safety-critical requests.

FROM AN INTERESTING RESULT TO A BETTER DESIGN CHOICE

What does this change
for people building AI?

For Data & AI leaders, the implication isn't to discourage ordinary courtesy. It's to design and evaluate systems around what people are trying to accomplish. That means asking whether an interaction really needs another generated reply, whether an answer resolved the task, and whether quality holds up across languages and models. Those are questions to test, not benefits this research has already proven.

Consider a wealth-management dealer whose advisors use AI to prepare for client meetings or explain complex information. A response that sounds polished may still omit something important; a shorter response may create more review and rework. The practical questions are whether the information is accurate, permissioned and understandable, and whether a qualified person remains accountable for advice and decisions. This is an illustrative application, not a finding about any particular firm or dealer.

The larger point: reducing unnecessary computation and improving human outcomes should be evaluated together—not treated as competing goals, or assumed to move in the same direction.

What if good AI begins by knowing
when it has done enough?

We began with two words that seemed too small to matter. They mean what they always meant. The question is whether we can design systems that know when another response helps—and when the most respectful response is to stop.
