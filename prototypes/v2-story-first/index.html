01 / THE MISMATCH

The conversation might already be over.

To another person, “thank you” often means the work is done. It's an acknowledgment, not a request to keep going.

But a conversational interface can treat that same message as another turn. It may read the earlier exchange again and generate another reply, even if no new answer was requested.

For us, a closing gesture. For the system, perhaps a fresh task.

That small difference is what sent me looking at the research. The interesting question isn't whether being courteous is wrong. It's whether the system has learned to distinguish a request from a goodbye.

iHow do we know?⌄

What researchers measured — Delavande, Pierrard and Luccioni reported a mean of 0.245 Wh across GPU, CPU and estimated RAM energy for generated thank-you replies in a specified Llama 3.1–8B/H100 experiment.

What it does not prove — It is not a universal ChatGPT cost, a water measurement or evidence that every reply is unnecessary.

PROTOTYPE B / ACT 02THE COURTESY PARADOX

THE SAME WORDS DON'T WORK THE SAME WAY EVERYWHERE

Does being polite
change the answer?

A thank-you after we're finished can invite another response. A polite request made during the work changes the task's wording. What does that change actually do?

We tend to imagine that courtesy is a simple setting: turn it up and people—and perhaps machines—will respond better. But language models don't necessarily follow that rule.

In a 2026 research preprint, five models were evaluated across English, Hindi and Spanish using different ways of phrasing requests. The results depended on which model, language and form of politeness the researchers examined.

Consider one English-language comparison for GPT-4o Mini. Under the study's scoring method, a direct request received a score of 0.636. A positively polite request received 0.567.

Those numbers aren't accuracy percentages. They're scores produced by the researchers' combined evaluation method. Still, the contrast complicates a familiar assumption: more polite wording does not always produce a higher evaluated score.

And this is only one pair of results. The fuller picture is more uneven.

iHow do we know?⌄

What is shown — PLUM (Mehta et al., 2026), Table IV, composite response-quality means for five models × three languages × five prompt categories.

What is not shown — These are automatically scored results, not independently validated task success or energy. Table V evaluates previous conversation histories separately; we do not invent their combined effect.

PROTOTYPE C / ACT 04THE LANGUAGE DIVIDE

WHAT THE AVERAGE LEAVES OUT

Not every language
gets the same deal.

If an AI system appears efficient on average, does that tell us what happens when someone uses it in a different language?

In one published benchmark, researchers measured the computation associated with outputs in 122 languages. Each language had its own result. The differences weren't random decoration around a single typical cost: some conditions required substantially more energy per generated token than others.

We can learn more by starting with the whole population, then changing how we look at it. The median tells us where the middle of the distribution lies. The mean is pulled toward unusually expensive observations. Both are useful; neither is everyone's experience.

A DIFFERENT QUESTION: WHAT COUNTS AS CORRECT?

STRICT SCORING
LENIENT SCORING

A response can fail strict formatting rules while still counting under lenient scoring. This is about the evaluation method, not a complete assessment of a language's users.

Lower-resource source group Higher-resource source groupGroups are source classifications, not quality or value judgements.

For this Qwen3–8B benchmark, the energy median is about 0.132 J per output token, while the mean is about 0.173 J. The mean is roughly 31% higher, suggesting that the “average” hides an expensive tail.

And computation isn't the only thing to examine. A scoring rule can change how capable a system appears. That's why a language-accuracy comparison should be read alongside the evaluation method, not mistaken for a judgment about a community.

Change the model, and the energy profile can change again. The complete research experience includes a separate twelve-language, three-model comparison. It is another study view—not evidence of the energy cost of politeness.
